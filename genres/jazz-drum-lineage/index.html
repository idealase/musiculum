Section 01

Executive summary

There is a family of music that has no settled genre name and does not need one. It is usually filed as jazz-funk, jazz rap, acid jazz, broken beat, nu jazz, or simply "the London scene", but those labels describe marketing categories rather than the thing itself. The thing itself is a drumming tradition — a fifty-year argument about where the beat sits, who is allowed to bend it, and how much imperfection a groove can hold before it stops being a groove.

The argument starts in the late 1960s, when a handful of American session drummers stopped functioning as timekeepers and started functioning as authors. Bernard Purdie, Clyde Stubblefield, Idris Muhammad, Zigaboo Modeliste, and Harvey Mason built parts that were not accompaniment but composition: ghost notes on the snare, hi-hat patterns with their own internal melody, a bass drum that answered the bass guitar rather than doubling it. In Lagos, Tony Allen was doing something parallel and arguably more radical, distributing a single pulse across four limbs so evenly that Fela Kuti's Afrobeat could run for twenty minutes without ever resolving. Fela's own summary is the shortest history of the style available: no Tony Allen, no Afrobeat.

The second act is the studio. Steely Dan hired those same players and treated the groove as a manufacturable object, cutting take after take until Purdie's half-time shuffle on Aja and Gaucho and Steve Gadd's dissolving solo on "Aja" sounded simultaneously loose and machined. That paradox — human feel captured with obsessive precision — is the sound a generation of producers would later go looking for in second-hand record shops.

The third act is the theft, and it is the most productive theft in modern music. Hip-hop producers bought those records for the four bars where the band dropped out, and in doing so turned a drummer's performance into a reusable grammar. A Tribe Called Quest, Pete Rock, Gang Starr, and Digable Planets built an entire aesthetic from upright bass and jazz-funk breaks; the loop preserved the swing of a 1973 drum take and put it in circulation forever.

The fourth act closes the circle. J Dilla programmed drums without quantisation, letting the snare land fractionally late in a way no machine would choose and no trained drummer had been permitted; Questlove and Chris Dave then learned to play that error deliberately. Once that skill existed, a live band could sound like a sampler, and the loop went back into human hands. Everything that follows — BadBadNotGood, Makaya McCraven, Yussef Dayes, Moses Boyd, Ezra Collective, Kokoroko — belongs to that reversal. The drummer, who spent the 1970s uncredited on the back of someone else's sleeve, ends up with their name on the front.

This guide is organised as six eras. It is not a jazz canon and it is not a hip-hop canon. It is a listening path through the records where the drums are the reason the record exists.

1969-1976: the drummer becomes the author
1972–1976
Section 02

1969-1976: the drummer becomes the author

Listen to almost any funk or soul-jazz record cut in New York between 1969 and 1974 and you are probably listening to a very small number of people. Bernard "Pretty" Purdie was on thousands of sessions, most famously as Aretha Franklin's musical director, and he brought a specific innovation: a half-time shuffle carried by triplet ghost notes so quiet they read as texture rather than notes. The pattern would be named after him, and it remains one of the few drum parts widely known by its inventor's name rather than the song it appears on.

He was not alone. Clyde Stubblefield's break on James Brown's "Funky Drummer" — a solo bar the band leaves him alone with — became one of the most sampled recordings ever made, and the second half of this guide is essentially the story of what happened to it. Idris Muhammad brought New Orleans second-line displacement into Blue Note and CTI sessions. Zigaboo Modeliste built the Meters' entire language out of syncopation and negative space. Harvey Mason gave Head Hunters and Black Byrd a rigid, danceable clarity that jazz purists distrusted and every subsequent producer loved.

Two other things happen in this window that matter enormously later. In Lagos, Tony Allen abandons the idea that any single limb carries the beat, producing a pulse that is felt collectively rather than counted — Afrobeat's real engine. And in New York, Miles Davis dispenses with song form entirely on On the Corner, treating the rhythm section as a bed of loops years before samplers made loops literal.

1974-1981: the shuffle enters the studio
1974–1980
Section 03

1974-1981: the shuffle enters the studio

Steely Dan is the hinge of this story, and not only because Becker and Fagen were jazz obsessives writing harmonically dense songs for a rock audience. They industrialised the session drummer. On The Royal Scam, Aja, and Gaucho they auditioned players track by track, replacing a part until the feel was exactly right — a process that produced both legendary results and a fair amount of resentment.

The most quoted outcome is Purdie's half-time shuffle on "Home at Last" and "Babylon Sisters": a groove so specific that Jeff Porcaro's famous adaptation for Toto's "Rosanna" openly acknowledges it. The other is Steve Gadd's performance on "Aja" itself, which turns a solo section into something closer to a controlled demolition, and which sits alongside his equally influential march-derived pattern on Paul Simon's "50 Ways to Leave Your Lover".

What matters historically is not the perfectionism but the artefact. This era produced immaculately recorded drums — isolated, close-miked, dry, and loud — attached to sophisticated jazz harmony. Those recordings would sit in second-hand shops for a decade until a generation with samplers found them and recognised exactly what they were: ready-made, high-fidelity grooves with the swing already baked in.

1988-1996: the break becomes a language
1988–1995
Section 04

1988-1996: the break becomes a language

By the late 1980s the records above were cheap, plentiful, and full of isolated drum bars. The SP-1200 and MPC60 made those bars loopable, and a strand of hip-hop emerged that treated jazz not as a style to imitate but as a source of raw rhythmic material with better drums than anything then being recorded.

A Tribe Called Quest's The Low End Theory is the pivot. It is not a jazz record — the group are rappers, and the album's minimalism is a hip-hop decision — but it hires Ron Carter to play upright bass, builds tracks from jazz-funk debris, and treats space as an instrument in a way that directly descends from the records in the previous section. Pete Rock, DJ Premier, Digable Planets, and Guru's Jazzmatazz project took the same premise in different directions, from dusty horn loops to full sessions with living jazz musicians.

Two consequences follow. First, an entire generation heard 1970s jazz-funk drumming through a filter of loops, filtered EQ, and deliberate dust — so the sound of the break, not just the pattern, became canonical. Second, the Roots proved a band could play it live without losing the loop's hypnotic sameness, which sets up everything in the last three sections of this guide.

1997-2006: the producer sits behind the kit
1998–2006
Section 05

1997-2006: the producer sits behind the kit

Two things happen at once in this period, and they are causally linked.

In West London, a scene later labelled broken beat took the swing of jazz-funk, the syncopation of drum and bass, and the harmony of soul, and produced rhythms with a permanent limp — deliberately uneven grids that no drummer would have played but which came out of an instinctively jazz-literate community. 4hero, Bugz in the Attic, and their Berlin cousins Jazzanova treated the sequencer as a jazz instrument. In Oslo, Jaga Jazzist did the equivalent with a big band.

In Detroit, meanwhile, J Dilla was making the crucial discovery. Programming an MPC with quantisation off, he placed hits where they felt right rather than where the grid demanded, producing beats that lurch and stagger while still grooving hard. Questlove heard this, sat behind a kit, and taught himself to reproduce it by hand — which is why Voodoo sounds like it is falling over and never does. That record, and Erykah Badu's Mama's Gun, transmitted the technique to a generation of musicians who had spent their childhoods being taught that playing behind the beat was a mistake.

Roy Hargrove's RH Factor is the moment jazz's institutional side formally acknowledged all of this: a trumpet player of impeccable straight-ahead credentials assembling a band around the neo-soul rhythm section instead of the other way round.

2006-2015: the Dilla feel goes live
2006–2015
Section 06

2006-2015: the Dilla feel goes live

Donuts was released three days before J Dilla died, and its influence is impossible to overstate: an album of unquantised, half-finished-sounding beats that a decade of conservatory-trained musicians would study as repertoire. What follows is a genuinely unusual historical reversal. Normally machines imitate people. Here, people spend ten years learning to imitate a machine being operated badly on purpose.

Chris Dave is the key executor. His playing on Robert Glasper's Black Radio takes the sagging, off-grid feel of programmed beats and performs it live with terrifying control, and the record's Grammy win pushed the approach into the jazz mainstream. Karriem Riggins, a drummer who also produces, works the same seam from the other end. Makaya McCraven goes further and inverts the process entirely: record long improvised sessions, then edit the tapes like a producer, so the finished album is a sampled record whose source material is his own band.

Meanwhile in Toronto, three students who started by playing Odd Future covers on jazz instruments became BadBadNotGood, and demonstrated that the whole lineage could be treated as one continuous repertoire rather than a set of separate traditions.

2016-present: the drummer leads the band
2016–2023
Section 07

2016-present: the drummer leads the band

The current era's defining feature is authorship. In South London especially, the bandleaders are drummers: Yussef Dayes, Moses Boyd, Femi Koleoso of Ezra Collective. Their training came through Tomorrow's Warriors and community music education rather than through American conservatories, and their reference points are grime, Afrobeat, dub, and jazz in roughly equal measure — which is why the rhythms sit differently from anything in the American tradition.

Black Focus is the record that announced it: Yussef Dayes and Kamaal Williams playing something that is recognisably fusion, recognisably broken beat, and recognisably a product of small sweaty London rooms. Sons of Kemet answered with twin drum kits and a tuba where the bass should be. Kokoroko took Tony Allen's Afrobeat directly rather than through American intermediaries. Ezra Collective, a band that plays like a party rather than a recital, won the Mercury Prize in 2023 — a decent marker of how completely the drummer-led band has moved from margin to centre.

The thread the user of this guide asked about is now closed. Purdie's ghost notes reach Steely Dan; Steely Dan's drum sound reaches the sampler; the sampler reaches Dilla; Dilla reaches Chris Dave and Questlove; and their feel reaches a generation of London and Los Angeles players who never needed to choose between jazz, hip-hop, and Afrobeat because they arrived after the distinction stopped being useful.

Listening routes

If you want a single evening rather than a full survey, use one of the four paths built into this guide.

If you only have seven evenings: Aja; Head Hunters; The Low End Theory; Voodoo; Donuts; Black Radio; Black Focus. That sequence contains the whole argument, in order, without a single redundant record.
